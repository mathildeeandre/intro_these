\section{Modeling complex populations with simple objects}

Population genetics studies how the genetic composition of a population changes
over generations under the combined effect of reproduction, random sampling and selection. 
The origins of discipline traces back to the early 19th century, when Jean-Baptiste Lamarck~\cite{de_monet_de_lamarck_philosophie_1809} proposed one of the first theories of evolution, based on the inheritance of characteristics acquired during an organism's lifetime. 20 years later, Charles Darwin developed a different explanation after an expedition in South America during which he observed the diversity of species and their geographical variation. In the late 1830s, he formulated the theory of natural selection as the main driver of the transformation and diversification of species~\cite{darwin_origin_1859}. 

In the 1960s, Motoo Kimura~\cite{kimura_evolutionary_1968} argued that the vast majority of mutations are neutral. According to this view, the genetic diversity observed in nature is shaped mainly by chance rather than by Darwinian natural selection. As a consequence, variation at the molecular level is governed by \emph{genetic drift}, which refers to the random change in the frequency of an allele in a population from one generation to the next.
The central question, which sparked a big controversy at the time, was the following:\\
\\
\emph{Can we explain all the observed polymorphism in natural populations without needing to invoke natural selection?}
\\
\\
This was the beginning of the so-call \textit{neutral theory of molecular evolution}, which provides the evolutionary framework on which many of the models used today are based.

The simplest setting in which this question can be posed
rigorously is that of a \emph{well-mixed} population with fixed size and in which every individual is, at each generation, equally likely to be the parent of any other individual. This model is called the Wright--Fisher model~\citep{wright_evolution_1931,fisher_genetical_1930} and serves as the basis to Kimura's neutral theory~\cite{kimura_evolutionary_1968}. 

This section recalls the classical descriptions of neutral, purely conservative population models, forward in time through models of reproduction, and backward in time through genealogies, and the universal objects, the Wright-Fisher diffusion
and the Kingman coalescent, that these descriptions converge to in the large population limit.
\paragraph{Notations.}
Throughout this work, we denote by $K$ a large demographic parameter that represents the size (or the order of magnitude of the size) of a population. For both discrete and continuous time models, we will denote by $t$ the time index, which can be either discrete or continuous. We will denote by $\mathcal{N}_t$ the set of individuals in the population at time $t$. The letter $z$ will refer to a notion of density (either allele frequencies or population densities in regulated models). 
\subsection{Discrete neutral population models}\label{sec:neutral_models}
\paragraph{Exchangeable models in discrete time.} Tracing back to the 1930 with the work of Wright~\cite{wright_evolution_1931} and Fisher~\cite{fisher_genetical_1930},
 the Wright-Fisher model is a stochastic model used as a baseline in evolutionary biology, population genetics and phylogenetics. It models the development of genetic variability within a population, explaining the genetic drift. In its simplest form, consider a population of $K$ individuals labeled $1,\dots,K$, each carrying one of two alleles, $A$ or $a$. The population is assumed to be well-mixed, meaning that every individual is equally likely to mate with any other individual. The model assumes discrete generations, where each generation is formed by sampling uniformly at random and with replacement a parent from the previous generation.

 An important assumption made here is the notion of \emph{exchangeability}: the law of the model vector is invariant under permutation of the labels of the individuals. Moreover, it is independent and identically distributed
across generations. 


 More generally, Cannings models~\cite{cannings_latent_1974,cannings_latent_1975} denote the family of discrete-generation, exchangeable population frameworks that generalize classical genetic models.
Fix $K \ge 2$. In the Cannings model, generations are indexed by $t \in \N$,
and each generation consists of $K$ individuals, labelled $1,\dots,K$. Individual
$i$ in generation $t$ produces $\nu_i(t) \ge 0$ offspring in generation $t+1$, and the progeny inherit the allele of their parent.

The offspring vector is denoted by
\[
\nu(t)\ \coloneqq\ (\nu_1(t),\dots,\nu_K(t)),\quad \text{such that}\quad \sum_{i=1}^K \nu_i(t)\ =\ K.
\] 
Cannings models is the most general class of neutral, exchangeable reproduction schemes with
fixed population size and non-overlapping generations, in the sense that no
further assumption is made on the offspring distribution beyond exchangeability
and the constraint $\sum_i \nu_i(t) = K$. The Wright-Fisher model is a particular instance of Cannings models, which corresponds to choosing the vector 
$\nu(t)$ to be distributed as a multinomial random vector:
\[
\forall t\in \N,\quad \nu(t)\ \sim\ \mathrm{multinom}(K; 1/K,\dots,1/K).
\]

\paragraph{Continuous-time exchangeable models.} 
Biological problems are usually modeled as continuous-time phenomena. 
The continuous-time analogue of the Wright-Fisher model is the Moran model. This is a continuous-time Markov chain in which individuals reproduce at independent exponential times, and each offspring replaces a uniformly chosen individual in the population. 

The Moran model extends naturally to non-binary reproduction events, in which a single individual can produce multiple offspring at once, replacing a random number of individuals in the population.  This model has been studied for instance in~\citet{etheridge_coalescent_2010,birkner_measure-valued_2009}. The evolution of the population is given by a resampling mechanism: each $d$-uplet of individuals dies after an exponential holding time with some positive rate $\lambda_d$. Upon death, one individual of the $d$-uplet is replaced by $d$ copies of themselves. This is called the $\Lambda$-Moran model.


\subsection{Universal scaling limits}
We introduce the two universal objects that emerge as scaling limits of the idealized discrete-time models presented in the previous section, and that will be used throughout this thesis.
\paragraph{Prospective limit: the Wright--Fisher diffusion.}
When the population size goes to infinity, the evolution of the frequency of a given allele in the Wright-Fisher model converges to a continuous-time, Gaussian object called the Wright-Fisher diffusion. It is a diffusion process on the unit interval $[0,1]$ that is the weak solution to the stochastic differential equation
\[
dZ_t\ =\ \sqrt{Z_t(1-Z_t)} dB_t,
\]
where $B_t$ is a standard Brownian motion. In an equivalent manner, the Wright--Fisher diffusion is described by its infinitesimal generator, which acts on twice continuously differentiable functions $f:[0,1]\to \R$ as
\[
\mathcal{G}_{WF} f(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{2} x(1-x) f''(x),\quad x\ \in\ [0,1].
\]
The Wright-Fisher diffusion is the scaling limit of the Moran model as well, and more generally of any Cannings model with finite offspring variance.

More generally, if we track the frequency of $k\geq 2$ neutral alleles in a population, then the joint evolution of the frequencies of these alleles (which we will refer to as \emph{neutral fractions} in Chapter~\ref{chp4_multitype}) is described in the large population limit by a diffusion process on the $k$-dimensional simplex $\Delta^{k}=\{x\in[0,1]^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i = 1\}$ called the $k$-allele Wright-Fisher diffusion. It is characterized by its infinitesimal generator, which acts on twice continuously differentiable functions $f:\R^{k}\to \R$ as
\[
 \mathcal{G}^k_{WF} f(\bm x)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^k x_i(\delta_{ij}-x_j) \partial_{i,j}f(\bm x), \quad \bm x\ =\ (x_1,\dots,x_k)\ \in\ \Delta^k.
\]

Adding selection to Cannings model with finite variance leads to a drift term in the Wright-Fisher diffusion, while modifying the variance of the offspring distribution changes the diffusion coefficient. 
\paragraph{Retrospective limit: neutral coalescent theory.}
An alternative way to describe the allelic composition of a population is to determine its genealogy. A genealogy is a forest structure that represents the degree of kinship
between individuals in a population. It is a central notion in population genetics, in particular because genealogies are instrumental in studying
patterns of neutral genetic diversity \citep{wakeley_coalescent_2009,
tellier2014coalescence}. Genealogies are a powerful inference tool to reconstruct a population's life history and genetic diversity. 
Indeed, in neutral models of population genetics, the standard statement is the following:
conditional on a genealogy, neutral mutations are superimposed independently on its branches according to Poisson processes with rate proportional to the branches' length~\cite{donnelly_coalescents_1995, griffiths_genealogy_2003}. 

Thus, the shape of the genealogies determines how many mutations we see and how frequent they are. Long branches imply more diversity, while shallow branching points imply that many individuals share a recent common ancestor, which in turn means that there is low diversity in the population. 

If we are interested in the genealogy of individuals sampled at a fixed ``present'' time, tracking the ancestral lineages backwards-in-time yields an (ultra)metric structure on the space of individuals alive at time $t$, equipped with the following \emph{genetic distance}
Fix $t>0$ and $k\geq 1$. We recall that ${\cal N}_t$ denotes the set of individuals alive at time $t$ in the forward-in-time population model.
For any pair $u,v \in{\cal N}_t$ with $u\neq v$, we denote by $D_{t}(u, v)$  the genetic distance between $u$ and $v$,  defined as the time to the death of their most recent common ancestor, see Figure~\ref{fig:distance_gen_intro}.

The mathematical theory of genealogies has been
mostly developed around the notion of coalescents, following the
seminal work of \cite{Kingman:1982aa} and its
subsequent generalizations \citep{Pitman:1999aa,
sagitov1999general, donnelly1999particle, schweinsberg2001coalescents}. 
A coalescent is a stochastic process obtained by sampling a finite number
of individuals at some reference generation, and following backward in
time their ancestral lineages until they merge (coalesce).
More precisely, conditional on the event $\{|{\cal N}_t|\geq k\}$, let 
$(U_1, \dots, U_k)$ be $k\geq 1$ individuals
sampled uniformly \emph{without replacement} from ${\cal N}_t$.

We introduce the process
$({\cal C}^{k}_{s,t})_{s \in [0, t]}$ with values in the partitions of
$\{1,\dots, k\}$ satisfying that
\[
    \forall \tau\leq t, \ \ i \sim_{{\cal C}^{k}_{s,t}} j\ \iff\
  D_{t}(U_i, U_j)\ \leq\  s.
\]
This procedure
constructs a stochastic process with
values in the partitions encoding the forest spanned by the sample $(U_i)_{i \leq k}$. 

\medskip

\paragraph{Exchangeable coalescents.}
In particular, the limiting genealogy of the Moran Model is described by a continuous-time Markov chain on the space of partitions of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ called the Kingman coalescent. It was introduced by Kingman in 1982~\cite{Kingman:1982aa}.
The Kingman coalescent with rate $r$ is the partition-valued process started from the singleton partition and such that each pair of blocks merge at rate $r$. 
Thus, the block-counting process is the decreasing integer-valued process with transitions
\[
n\ \longrightarrow\ n-1 \qquad \text{at rate}\quad r\binom{n}{2}.
\]

It was established in the work of~\citet{mohle_1998} and ~\citet{sagitov1999general} that the Kingman coalescent is also the limit of the genealogies in Cannings models, if the variance of the offspring distribution rescaled by the population size has a finite limit. That is if,
\[
\lim_{K\to\infty }\frac{1}{K}\E\big[\nu_1(t)(\nu_1(t)-1)\big]\ =\ m_2<\infty.
\]
Under this assumption, the genealogies converge to the Kingman coalescent with merging rate $m_2$ on the timescale $K$. The genetic diversity of the population is captured by this pairwise merging rate of the coalescent, and thus to the variance of the offspring distribution in the forward-in-time model. We will develop this notion of  \emph{effective population size} in the next section. 


However, if the variance of the offspring distribution is infinite, then the Kingman coalescent can no longer be a good estimate for the genealogies of the population. Notably, we say that a probability distribution $L$ with values in $\R_+$ is heavy-tailed if its tail probability $\Prob(L > x)$ decays slower than any exponential as $x\to\infty$

In particular, it has a power-law tail if there exists $\alpha \in (1,2)$ such that
\[
\Prob(L > x)\ \sim\ C x^{-\alpha} \quad \textit{as}\quad x\ \to\ \infty.
\]

If the offspring distribution per individual in a Cannings model is heavy-tailed, then a single individual can, with non-negligible probability, produce a positive fraction of the whole next generation.
Zooming out, these large birth events create big families who persist in the large population limit. Looking at the genealogies, individuals at present are likely to descend from these large families, so that the diversity of the population is reduced and the genealogy of the population is no longer described by a binary tree like the Kingman coalescent, but rather by a tree with multiple mergers. 

Pitman~\cite{Pitman:1999aa} and Sagitov~\cite{sagitov1999general} characterized the class of exchangeable coalescent processes admitting multiple mergers. That is, merging events involving several blocks, but at most one merger event at a time. Given a finite measure $\Lambda$ on $[0,1]$, the $\Lambda$-coalescent is the Markov chain on the space of partitions of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with the following dynamics:
 Starting from the singleton partition, if there are $b$ blocks present, any given group of $k$ of them $(2 \leq k \leq b)$ merges into one at rate
\[
\lambda_{b,k}\ =\ \int_0^1 x^{k-2} (1-x)^{b-k}\, \Lambda(dx),
\]
independently of the other groups.  Taking $\Lambda = \delta_0$ in the above display recovers the Kingman coalescent. In the beginning of the century, Möhle and Sagitov~\cite{mohle_classification_2001,Mohle_Sagitov} provided the exact conditions on the offspring distribution of a Cannings model under which its genealogy, once suitably rescaled, converges to a $\Lambda$-coalescent. 
In particular, if the law of $\nu_1$ has a power tail with parameter $\alpha$, then the genealogies converge to a $\Lambda$-coalescent with measure $\Lambda=\mathrm{Beta}(1-\alpha, \alpha)$.
Thereby, they classified the possible scaling limits of the genealogies in exchangeable populations with fixed size.

\paragraph{Duality} 
Duality is a fundamental concept in mathematics and takes many forms. The underlying idea is to control a complex object through a simpler one, which may take values in a different space. In population models, Markov duality is a powerful tool for connecting two stochastic processes. It often relates the forward-in-time evolution of allele frequencies to a backward-in-time process describing genealogical quantities, such as the number of ancestral lineages.

This forward-backward correspondence is reflected in the scaling limits of the classical population models that we described before. In particular, the Wright--Fisher diffusion and the Kingman coalescent can be seen as two sides of the same coin. They describe, respectively, the forward-in-time and backward-in-time scaling limits of the Wright--Fisher and Moran models. More generally, if the neutral allele frequencies in a population model converge forward in time to the Wright--Fisher diffusion, one expects the genealogies to converge to the Kingman coalescent. 
Likewise, if they converge to a $\Lambda$-Fleming--Viot process, one expects the genealogies to converge to an exchangeable coalescent with multiple mergers.

This heuristic has been rigorously established only within certain classes of models, such as Cannings models~\cite{mohle_convergence_1998,Mohle_Sagitov}. For neutral exchangeable populations, the forward and backward descriptions are linked by a \emph{moment duality} formula.
The moment duality between the Wright--Fisher diffusion and the Kingman coalescent is arguably the most famous instance of duality in population genetics. Let $X_t \in [0,1]$ denote the frequency of an allele at time $t$ in the Wright--Fisher diffusion, and let $N(t)$ denote the number of blocks at time $t$ in the Kingman coalescent. Then
\[
\E_{x}\big[X_t^{n}\big]\ =\ \E_n\big[x^{ N(t)}\big].
\]
We refer to \cite{etheridge_mathematical_2011,ethier_1986, mohle_concept_1999, carinci_dualities_2015, cordero_bernstein_2026, blath_separation_2021,etheridge_coalescent_2010} for other forms of duality in population genetics.
Despite these links, there is no general method for deducing the distribution of the genealogy directly from the forward-in-time dynamics. For most models, as highlighted in~\cite{Julie_Zsofia}, this remains a technical challenge.

 
\section{Central questions in applied population genetics}

\subsection{Lewontin's paradox and the effective population size}


Genetic drift was demonstrated experimentally in the PhD thesis of Buri~\cite{buri_gene_1956}, who followed populations of \emph{Drosophila melanogaster}. Over just a few generations, allele frequencies drifted randomly, and many populations ended up fixed for one allele or the other. Importantly, the observed loss of heterozygosity was faster than predicted by the Wright--Fisher model. This means that the populations behaved as if they were smaller. This result illustrates a great paradox in population genetics.


Neutral theory and the class of unstructured exchangeable models assume that in a population of size $N$, diversity results from an equilibrium between new mutations arising at a constant rate $\mu$ and genetic drift that purge them at a rate $1/N$. Thus, according to neutral models, the predicted quantitative  value for genetic diversity at equilibrium should be proportional to $N\mu$. The discrepancy between the neutralist's expectation and the much lower observed levels of genetic diversity in real populations is known as the Lewontin paradox~\cite{lewontin_genetic_1974, leffler_revisiting_2012}.


\paragraph{The effective population size}
Wright \cite{wright_evolution_1931} proposed to account for such discrepancies, and more generally for any departure from the idealized Wright-Fisher setting, by
introducing an \emph{effective population size}, which is classically denoted by $N_e$ in the litterature.
The effective Population Size captures the number of individuals that effectively participates in producing the next generation. It reflects how much genetic drift a real population experiences, hence its rate of loss of genetic variability. It accounts for parameters such as demographic fluctuations, variance in reproductive success, population structure, sex ratio, linked selection, etc. These parameters greatly influence evolutionary processes.
 Generally, the effective size of a population is considerably less than its census size,  with a $1:1$ ratio only in the special case of an ``ideal'' panmictic population, in which variation among individuals in reproductive success is completely random. 

Lewontin's paradox illustrates why the effective population size, rather than the number of individuals, is the relevant quantity in evolutionary biology for understanding genetic diversity (even if it should be pointed out that $N_e$ is not sufficient to resolve the paradox, as can be seen \eg in~\citet{buffalo_quantifying_2021}). It has been widely used across scientific fields and has received considerable attention over the past decades. Estimating $N_e$ is crucial for conservation and biodiversity monitoring, \eg to classify and protect endangered species. Populations with low genetic diversity are more vulnerable to disease, climate change, and other stressors, and may be at risk of extinction even when their census size is large~\cite{mergeay_importance_2026, frankham_effective_1995}.
However, it does not have a universal and constant in time definition: it was initially introduced in the 1930s by Sewall Wright~\cite{wright_evolution_1931} as follows:
\\
\\
\textit{
  ``The number of surviving offspring left by different parents may vary tremendously either through selection or merely accidental causes, a condition which tends to reduce the effective N far below the actual number of parents or even of grandparents.''
}--\citet{wright_evolution_1931}
\\
\\
From this definition, $N_e$ is the size of an idealized well-mixed Wright--Fisher population whose rate of genetic drift, measured for instance by the pairwise coalescence time or by the rate of loss of heterozygosity, matches that of the population under study. 

Several, non-equivalent, definitions of $N_e$ have since been developed. 
For example, using the change of inbreeding (see~\citet{crow_inbreeding_1988}), the variance in reproductive success, 
the coalescent rate of lineages backward in time (see~\citet{Kingman:1982aa}), the linkage disequilibrium (that is, the nonrandom association between alleles at different loci~\cite{slatkin_linkage_2008}), the sibling relationships among sampled individuals, etc.
We refer to Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth_effective_2009,charlesworth_how_2022} and Waples~\cite{waples_what_2022,waples_idiots_2025} for reviews over the different definitions of this concept. To each definition of $N_e$ corresponds a principle of inferring it.
In Chapter~\ref{chp3_singletype} and Chapter~\ref{chp4_multitype} of this thesis, we will use the definition of $N_e$ that relates to coalescent theory: the pairwise merging rate $r$ of linages in a Kingman coalescent relates to the effective population size through
\[
r\ =\ \frac{1}{N_e}.
\]

\subsection{Neutral models in practice}
Neutral models and the whole class of unstructured, exchangeable models presented in the last paragraphs all rest on idealizations, constant population size, panmixia and neutrality of reproduction. Essentially, no natural population satisfies these assumptions exactly.


Despite this observation, neutral theory remains the default null model used in applied population genetics, because it yields explicit predictions that can be confronted with data.

For instance, under the Kingman coalescent with the infinite-sites mutation model (every new mutation occurs at a novel site, and there is no recombination), the expected number of mutations carried by $k$ of $n$ sampled individuals has an explicit form, which is the basis of classical estimator of the mutation rate of Watterson and of the neutrality tests such as the estimators of Tajima~\cite{tajima_statistical_1989} and Fu and Li~\cite{Fu_Li_Neutrality_tests}.
The site-frequency spectrum (SFS) counts for each $k$ the mutations carried by exactly $k$ sampled individuals. It is a widely used summary of genetic variation whose expectation is proportional to the total length of the branches supporting $k$ leaves~\cite{wakeley_coalescent_2009}. Other statistical and coalescent-based methods for reconstructing the evolutionary history of DNA sequences are reviewed in~\citet{griffiths_ancestral_2001}.

The Kingman coalescent model also allows to infer the demographic history of a population. Measuring time in generations under the Kingman coalescent, if during the $i^{th}$ inter-coalescence interval, $n_i$ lineages are present, the waiting time to the next coalescence is approximately exponential with mean $N_e(t)/\binom{n_i}{2}$. The effective population size at time $t$ can be read directly off the observed branch lengths of the tree (see figure~\ref{fig:skyline_plot}). This method is called the \emph{skyline plot} and was introduced by \cite{pybus_integrated_2000}. It is used routinely to reconstruct the demographic history of viral epidemics and other rapidly evolving pathogens \citep{pybus_evolutionary_2009,ho_skyline-plot_2011}, none of which reproduce according to literal Wright-Fisher dynamics. It works because, as recalled above, the Kingman coalescent is the common scaling limit of every Cannings model with finite offspring variance, so the $N_e$ it returns remains a meaningful summary of genetic diversity regardless of the true, unknown reproduction mechanism within the population.

The approach breaks down when reproduction is itself strongly skewed, epidemic superspreading, selective sweeps, oversampling, which produce multifurcating genealogies that the almost surely binary Kingman coalescent cannot generate; here it is again a simple, exchangeable model, the $\Lambda$-coalescent, that is used to extend the skyline plot and recover the ability to infer demographic history \citep{hoscheit_multifurcating_2019}. 


Exchangeable coalescents (Kingman, $\Lambda$-coalescents) and neutral models are the standard null hypothesis for modeling complex populations.
Yet, does it still make sense to use these processes to explain genetics diversity in the wild? The answer given by mathematics is broadly positive. 


\paragraph{Robustness of the Kingman coalescent within single type populations: motivation for Chapter~\ref{chp1_single_type}}
We saw that, after a suitable scaling, the Wright--Fisher diffusion and the Kingman coalescent appear as the (forward and backward) scaling limits of Cannings models with finite variance and selection. 
Besides, the Kingman coalescent has been proved to be robust to modifications from the tight assumptions of the Moran model and the Cannings model with variance. Indeed, it arises as the genealogy of a broad class of forward-in-time population models. Beyond the purely neutral setting presented in Section~\ref{sec:neutral_models}, the Kingman coalescent process has served as the backbone onto which further biological ingredients are grafted. For instance, variation of population size over time is incorporated by a deterministic time change of the coalescent rate
\citep{griffiths_sampling_1994, Jagers2004ConvergenceTT}, or by a stochastic time change in models with stochastically varying population size~\cite{kaj_coalescent_2003}.
Exchangeable coalescents also appear as the limiting genealogies of population models with selection \citep{kaplan_coalescent_1988, neuhauser_genealogy_1997}, recombination \citep{hudson_coalescent_1988}, diploid biparental models \citep{wakeley_gene_2012, koskela_robust_2019}, non-excheangeable models~\citep{siri-jegousse_exchangeable_2024}, etc. 
We also refer to~\cite{Brown:2023aa} for non-neutral models converging to the Kingman coalescent. 


Here, we can draw a parallel between the Wright-Fisher diffusion and other Gaussian objects that are universal scaling limits in probability. The simplest instance is that of \iid Bernoulli experiments. The rescaled number of heads in a sequence of perfect coin tosses converges to a Gaussian random variable. Variation of this experiment, for instance by adding a bias to the coin, leads to a re-scaling of the same object by a drift term. Likewise, it is well established by Donsker's theorem that the Brownian motion is the universal scaling limit of random walks with finite variance. The symmetric random walk converges to the standard Brownian motion, while other random walks with finite variance converge to a rescaling of this universal Gaussian object.

In the same way, current research works show that the Wright-Fisher diffusion and the Kingman coalescent are the universal scaling limit of neutral populations with finite variance. Population with skewed reproduction, in which a single individual can produce a positive fraction of the next generation, converge to a different class of universal objects, the $\Lambda$-Wright-Fisher diffusion and $\Lambda$-coalescents.

In Chapter~\ref{chp3_single_type}, we extend the universality class of the Kingman coalescent to density-dependent individual-based population models.

\section{Universality in structured populations}
A lot of recent research works indicate that not taking into account the structure of a population seriously misrepresents the estimate of changes in effective population size over time, and thus of the population's genetic diversity. A structured population can mimic the genetic signature of a population decline (bottleneck) or a completely fictitious event of interbreeding (see~\citet{mazet_importance_2016}), so that understanding the play of the ecology is crucial to understanding the genetic diversity of a population. 
Movement of population can take several forms across species. For example, one can think of the seasonal migrations observed in some populations, in particular within birds (barnacle geese, storks, etc.). Movement can also be initiated by density-denpendent competition and the need to explore new inhabitats in search of resources. 

Within interacting populations, the genealogies of structured populations is established in full generality only for a very restricted class of models called \emph{meta-population} models. Extending these results to more involved structured models presents notable challenges.

\subsection{State of the art: meta-populations models}
Meta-population refer to models where individuals are partitioned into a finite size of fixed size islands ({\it no demographic fluctuations}), which are connected through migration. 
Meta-population models are purely genetic and conservative models, with no demography: the offspring numbers of different individuals are anti-correlated in the sense that every birth is compensated by a death. 
 It extends the class of monotype fixed size models such as the class of Cannings models and the Moran model. 
 We give a few examples of such celebrated models, and refer to~\citet{etheridge_mathematical_2011} for a broader review.
 
\paragraph{Prospective picture: island models.}
The first and most elementary representative of the class of meta-population models is Wright's island model \cite{wright_isolation_1943}. Under this model, the population is modeled as a complete graph in which the vertices (the demes) are connected by edges. The population size is fixed and  spread across the demes. Inside each deme, the population reproduce according to a Wright--Fisher dynamic, except from a small proportion of pairs of migrants which are picked uniformly at random and exchange positions. 
We can think of more involved island models. For instance, a spatially structured population on a plane can be approximated by an island structure on the lattice.
However, a feature shared by almost all of
these models is that the population size is fixed, and the number of individuals of each type is given. 

Taking the infinite population limit, we obtain the \emph{stepping-stone} model of~\citet*{kimura_stepping_1964}, which is a diffusion approximation of metapopulations. This model assume a population partitioned into a finite number of islands, each of them having inhabitants subdivided into two allelic types $a$ and $A$. Individuals from island $i$ migrate to island $j$ with rate $m_{ij}$, under the further condition that $\sum_{j\neq i}m_{ji}=\sum_{j\neq i}m_{ij}$, so that population within demes is constant.
The proportion of individuals with allele $a$ in a given island is solution to a SDE called the Wright--Fisher diffusion with migration.


\paragraph{Retrospective picture: the structured coalescent}

The genealogies of meta-population models are given by the  \emph{structured coalescent}
\citet{takahata_coalescent_1988,notohara_coalescent_1990}, which extends the classical Kingman coalescent~\cite{Kingman:1982aa} by adding a finite type structure to lineages. Lineages migrate between demes and within-demes coalescence occurs at rate given by the inverse of the local population size. 

More precisely, let $D,k\geq 1$. For $j\neq i\in \{1,\ldots,D\}$, let and $N_j$ be the local population size in deme $j$ and $m_{ij}$ the (backward in time) migration rate of a lineage from $i$ to $j$. From there, the structured $k$-coalescent with $D$ demes has the following dynamics.
Each sampled lineage $p\in\{1,\ldots, k\}$ is assigned an initial deme $d_p \in [D]$. 
Then for all $i,j\in\{1,\ldots, D\}$ each pair of lineages in deme $i$ coalesces at rate $\lambda_i=1/N_i$ and each lineage in deme $i$ jumps to deme $j$ at rate $m_{ij}$. We can encode this process as a process on the set of marked partitions of $\{1,\ldots,k\}$, that is, a partition of $\{1,\ldots,k\}$ in which every block carry an additional type (here deme label).
The block-counting process of the structured coalescent with $D$ demes is the process taking values in $\N^D$ and such that if $n_i$ counts the number of lineages in deme $i$, the transitions are given by
\begin{align*}
n_i\ &\longmapsto\ n_i-1\qquad \text{at rate }\binom{n_i}{2}\lambda_i,\\
n_i\ &\longmapsto\ n_i+1  \qquad\text{and}\qquad n_j\ \longmapsto\ n_j-1 \qquad \text{at rate }m_{ji} n_j.\\
\end{align*}

The structured coalescent is dual, in the sense introduce before, to the
forward-in-time stepping-stone model \cite[Chapter~4]{etheridge_mathematical_2011}.
This duality extends to Cannings-type reproduction within each deme, yielding
multitype exchangeable coalescents in which the deme sizes are themselves random
(see~\citet*{huillet_asymptotic_2021,mohle_multi-type_2024}).


\subsection{Density dependence and constrained populations}

Despite being tractable, the lack of demographic fluctuations in meta-populations do not model appropriately ecological interactions as well as many important features of biological populations. For instance,
they can hardly model individuals invading a spatial continuum or
with a continuous age structure.


Conversely, density-dependent branching processes
is another well-studied class of models which seems suited to model such
phenomena. In these models, individuals reproduce independently, but in a
way that depends on the state of the population and which can model biological
interactions. For population without structure, the logistic process of~\citet{Lambert2005} is the typical example of a density-dependent branching process. 

When it comes to structured, interacting population models that allows for demography like density dependent branching particle systems, the picture for the ancestral lineages is less clear.
 This provides motivation for extending our knowledge
of genealogies to these processes, beyond models with fixed population
structure.
Two questions arise naturally.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(Q1)] First, how can the genealogy be described for a finite population?
\item[(Q2)]  Second, when the population is large, what can we say about the limiting genealogy? Does a universal genealogy  arise at the limit? 
\end{itemize}  
In the next paragraphs describe a few examples of density-dependent population models with a type structure and what is known on the ancestral lineages.

\paragraph{Density-dependent populations in finite and countable type spaces.}
In Chapter~\cite{chp4:multitype}, we study the following structured density-dependent particle model in continuous time. Let $E$ be a finite type space and consider a set of particles, each of them being endowed with a type. 
The state of the population is described through its \emph{density}, denoted by 
 $z\in \R_+^E$. By ``density'', we mean the vector counting the number of particles in each type, divided by a large scaling parameter $K>0$. We think of the parameter $K$ as the typical number of individuals in the population, which we call \emph{carrying capacity} of the system. If the state of the population is given by $z$, each of the $Kz_x$ particles with type $x$ die at rate that depends on $x$ and on $z$ and is replaced upon death by a random vector of offspring, whose types at birth are distributed according to a random variable which distribution, again, depends on $x$ and $z$. This procedure describes a population model iteratively.

In this framework,~\cite{Kurtz:1978aa} established a law of large number for the rescaled count of types in the population, under some regularity assumptions 
\begin{prop}{\citet[Theorem~2.2]{Kurtz:1978aa}}
  Assume that $\lim_{K\to\infty}Z_0\eqqcolon z_0$ exists almost-surely in $\R_+^E$ and denote by
  $(z_t)_{t\geq 0}$ the solution to the $E$-dimensional dynamical system 
  \begin{equation*}
\dot z_t\ =\ z_t M(z_t),
\end{equation*}
with initial condition $z_0$.
Assume that $z\mapsto zM(z)$ is Lipchitz and that for all $n\in\N^E$, $\exists\ C_n>0$ such that $\sum_n \lVert n\rVert C_n<\infty$ and
\[
\sup_{x\in E, z\in \R_+^E} z_x q_x(z) \Prob(L_x(z)=n)\ \leq\ C_n(1+ \lVert z\rVert).
\]
Then the limit
\[
\adjustlimits{\lim}_{K\to\infty}{\sup}_{t\leq T} \big|Z_t-z_t\big| \ \longrightarrow\ 0
\]
holds in probability, for every $T>0$.
\end{prop}
Moreover, under additional assumptions on the offspring distribution, Kurtz also established a central limit theorem for the fluctuations of the rescaled count of types around the deterministic limit. In particular, he showed that these fluctuations converge to an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process (see \cite[Theorem 4.4]{Kurtz:1978aa}), at a speed $\sqrt{K}$.

 In the countable type space setting,~\citet{Bansaye} studied an individual-based model with density-dependent interaction, and retrieved 
the empirical distribution of the types along a typical surviving lineage.

\paragraph{Regulated population on a continuum.}


When one thinks about continuous type space, the epitomic class is that of spatially structured models. In these models, individuals occupy positions, disperse locally, and compete mainly with nearby individuals. In the Bolker--Pacala model~\cite{bolker1997using}, individuals move in space according to a random walk and die at a rate that depends on the local density, which models competition for resources around their location. Competition is thus nonlocal in space rather than proportional to the total population size. Such local regulation can stabilize the population and generate clustering phenomena. Fournier and Méléard~\cite{Fournier_2004}, followed by~\citet{Champagnat_2006}, developed rigorous microscopic frameworks for density-dependent branching populations in which individuals occupy spatial positions, disperse locally, and experience competition whose strength depends on nearby density. We also point out the work of~\citet{alison_m_etheridge_looking_2024}, who studied spatial population models on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with density-dependent birth and death rates and, using a lookdown construction, described the motion of ancestral lineages in the deterministic scaling limit.

A different kind of spatial structure is found in the Brunet--Derrida $N$-particle model~\cite{brunet_shift_1997,brunet_effect_2007}, a branching--selection particle system in which particles branch and diffuse, but only the rightmost $N$ particles survive, so that the population forms a traveling front. Although its spatial structure is organized around a moving front rather than local ecological competition, it is a canonical example of how selection and finite population size reshape spatially expanding branching populations.

Beyond spatial structure, other models use the continuous type to encode a trait under selection. \citet*{forien_ancestral_2022} used neutral fractions to study genealogies in a one-dimensional SDE model with competition and selection, in which the population adapts to a moving environmental optimum. An individual-based version of this model was studied in~\cite{meleard-tran}. Motivated by climate change, \citet{meleard-tran} considered a branching-diffusion model in which each individual carries a one-dimensional trait $x\in\mathbb{R}$. At time $t$, an individual with trait $x$ gives birth at rate 1 to a child who inherits the parent's trait, and dies at rate $(x-ct)^2/2 + Z_t$, where $Z_t$ is the density of the population (the population size rescaled by a carrying capacity $K$). The first part of the death rate reflects an optimal trait moving at constant speed $c$, modeling a gradual environmental change; the second is a logistic competition term. Between reproduction and death events, the traits of individuals diffuse as independent Brownian motions. As $K\to\infty$, the empirical measure converges to a nonlinear nonlocal PDE. Using a many-to-one formula, the authors' main result is that the ancestral trait path of an individual sampled uniformly at time $T$, read backwards in time, converges to an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process. This process is independent of the speed $c$ of the environmental change and is centered at the optimum.

\paragraph{Universality and collapse of the structure}

In continuity of the work established in Chapter~\ref{chp3_single_type} to extend the robustness of the Kingman coalescent to, our goal in Chapter~\ref{chp4_multitype} is to extend the universality class of the Kingman coalescent to a broad class of structured density-dependent individual-based models. In particular, we investigate the play of structure on the genetic diversity through the pairwise merging rate of the limiting Kingman coalescent.

 The universality class of the Kingman coalescent is already known to contain several families of structured models, which we briefly review below.

In meta-population models, \cite{nordborg_separation_2002} proved that when migration events occur on a much faster timescale than coalescences, there is a separation of timescales leading to a \emph{slow--fast principle}: on the slow evolutionary timescale, genealogies are given by the Kingman coalescent with a rescaled effective population size (see also \cite{nagylaki_strong-migration_1980, wilkinson-herbots_coalescence_2003}). The spatial structure therefore collapses, and the subdivision into demes affects diversity only through this effective population size. We recover the same behavior in our main result for regulated populations on finite type spaces (see Theorem~\ref{thm:main_multitype}).
A similar collapse of the spatial structure occurs in models with a continuous space. The spatial $\Lambda$-Fleming--Viot process is not a particle system but a measure-valued model, obtained as the infinite-intensity limit of an individual-based model driven by a Poisson point process of reproduction events. \cite{barton_new_2010} proved that, in the regime where reproduction events affect only a negligible fraction of the habitat, the genealogy of a sample converges to the Kingman coalescent. This model therefore also belongs to the universality class of the Kingman coalescent.


The universality class of the Kingman coalescent also contains some specific structured particle models with density-dependent interactions. In particular,~\cite{etheridge_genealogies_2022} considered a spatial (one-dimensional) individual-based Moran model of a diploid population with selection against heterozygotes, which gives rise to a bistable wave (that is, a strong Allee effect in ecological terms). They rigorously proved, using a model-specific argument based on tracer dynamics, that the genealogy of a sample taken near the front of the wave converges to a Kingman coalescent.

\paragraph{Motivation for Chapter~\ref{chp4_multitype}}

More generally, it is conjectured that the genealogies of many population models converge to exchangeable coalescents, such as the $\Lambda$-coalescents \citep{Pitman:1999aa,sagitov1999general}.

Our motivation in Chapter~\ref{chp4_multitype} came from formalizing this conjecture for a broader class of density-dependent individual-based models. In particular, we were motivated by spatially structured populations described by reaction-diffusion equations, such as the stochastic Fisher--KPP equation. Reaction-diffusion PDEs take the form
\[
\partial_t u \ =\ \frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 u + f(u),
\]
where the reaction term $f(u)$ models local interactions between individuals. In the F--KPP equation, $f(u)=u(1-u)$ accounts for local competition for resources (density dependence). Other choices of $f$ model other interactions. For instance, an Allee effect (cooperation between individuals) is obtained with $f(u)=u(1-u)(1+Bu)$, where $B>0$ reflects the strength of the Allee effect.
To study the genealogies of such populations, \cite{hallatschek_gene_2008} introduced neutral fractions, \ie heritable neutral markers whose frequencies evolve with the population. The nature of the genealogies depends strongly on the type of front.
 For $f(u)=u(1-u)(1+Bu)$, the front is pulled for $B\le 2$ and pushed for $B>2$ (see \cite{birzu_fluctuations_2018}). In pulled fronts, which are driven by the few individuals at the tip of the expanding front wave, the genealogies are expected to be given by the Bolthausen--Sznitman $\Lambda$-coalescent. This result was predicted for the Brunet--Derrida $N$-particle system of \cite{brunet_effect_2007} and proved for branching Brownian motion with absorption in \cite{berestycki_genealogy_2013}. 
In pushed fronts, which are driven by the bulk of the population, one expects Kingman-type genealogies, as proved for a bistable wave by \cite{etheridge_genealogies_2022}.



In light of the preceding paragraph, this thesis is motivated by a single conjecture about universality in population genetics:
\\

\emph{Large populations that are structured and regulated become indistinguishable from a well-mixed Wright--Fisher population, provided the parameters are suitably rescaled. Two quantities carry this rescaling: an effective population size $N_e$, and an effective selection coefficient $s_e$, which captures how the interplay of structure, regulation, and selection alters the fate of an allele.
}
\\

Our aim in~\ref{chp4_multitype} is to move toward this statement and to identify general conditions under which the genetic diversity of a population can be described by a rescaling of neutral models, even when its ecology is complex. Specifically, we ask when a structured, interacting population still behaves on the coalescent timescale as though it were well mixed, with some effective size $N_e$.



\section{Universality in branching processes}
As we emphasized so far, little is known about the genealogies of most regulated, individual-based models used in population dynamics, except in particular cases where the ecology is trivial or when a duality formula links the forward and backward dynamics.

The situation is much better for branching models, such as the multitype Galton--Watson process and continuous-time branching processes. In these branching models, individuals reproduce and move in the type space independently of one another. From the mathematicians' point of view, neglecting all interactions between individuals offers a great simplification that allows to derive precise results for the large population behavior, forward and backwards in times. We briefly introduce some of the branching models that will be used throughout this thesis. We also recall some important limit theorems for branching processes and their genealogies, as well as  methodologies used to derived them, and that will be extended in the next three chapters of this work. 

\subsection{Branching processes with a finite set of types}
\paragraph{Multitype branching population models.}
In this paragraph, we rely to the monograph of~\citet*{athreya_branching_1972}.  In a first place, we recall the Single-type Galton--Watson process, introduced by Bienaymé~\cite{Bienayme1845, heyde_studies_1972}, then 30 years later by Galton and Watson~\cite{watson_probability_1875} to study the persistence of noble family names.
\begin{defn}[Single-type (Bienaymé)--Galton--Watson]
    Let $L$ be an integer valued random variable.
    Denote by $(Z_n)_{n\geq 0}$ the single-type Galton--Watson process with offspring distribution $L$. It is constructed recursively by setting $Z_0=1$ and for all $n\geq 0$,
    \[  
    Z_{n+1}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{Z_n} L^{(i,n)}.
    \]
    where the $(L^{(i,n)})_{i, n\in \N}$ are \iid copies of the random variable $L$. 
\end{defn}
In this thesis, we will work under the classical assumption that the mean number of offspring per individual is finite, that is $\E[L]<\infty$.

\medskip

Adding a type structure, let $E$ denote a finite type space.
The multitype Galton--Watson model is defined from a fixed family of distributions
  $(\mathcal{L}_x)_{x\in E}$ on $\mathbb{N}^{E}$.
  For each $x,y \in E$, for a random variable
  $L_x\sim \mathcal{L}_x$, we write $L_{xy}$ for its
  $y$-marginal. 
\begin{defn}\label{def:GW}
     Let us fix some $Z_0\in \mathbb{N}^{E}$.

    The mulitype Galton--Watson process with offspring
  distributions $(\mathcal{L}_x)_{x\in E}$ and started from
  $Z_0$ is defined as a sequence of $\mathbb{N}^{E}$-valued
  random variables $(Z_n)_{y\geq 0}$ satisfying the induction:
  \[
    Z_{n+1}\ =\ \sum_{x\in E} \sum_{i=1}^{Z_n(x)} L_t^{(i,n)},
    \qquad n\geq 0,
  \]
  where $(L_x^{(i,n)})_{x\in E, i\geq 1, n\geq 0}$ is a
  collection of independent random variables, with
  $L_x^{(i,n)}\sim \mathcal{L}_x$.
\end{defn}
The random variable $Z_n(x)$ models the number of type-$y$
  individuals in the $n^{th}$ generation of a population in which
  individuals of any given type $x\in E$ reproduce
  independently of the others with a progeny distributed as the random
  variable $L_x$. 

Once again, we assume that the first moment
  $M_{xy}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[L_{xy}]$ is finite,
so that the \emph{mean matrix} defined coordinate-wise by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean_matrix_intro}
\forall x,y\in E\quad\ M_{xy}\ \coloneqq\  \E\big[L_{xy}\big] 
\end{equation}
is well-defined.



The mean matrix $M$ introduced in~\eqref{eq:mean_matrix_intro} is nonnegative. It can be seen as the transition matrix of a discrete time Markov chain with finite states. It is said to be \emph{irreducible} and \emph{aperiodic} if the Markov chain with transitions $M$ is. Under these two assumptions, the mean asymptotic behavior of multitype Galton--Watson processes is governed by the Perron--Frobenius theorem.

\begin{thm}[Perron--Frobenius]
 If $M$ is a nonnegative, irreducible and aperiodic matrix, then there exists a principal eigenvalue $\rho>0$, and a unique couple of associated left and right eigenvectors denoted by $h$ and $\tilde h$ such that
    \[
    Mh\ =\ \rho h,\quad \tilde h M \ =\ \rho\tilde h,\quad \langle \tilde h, h\rangle \ =\ \langle \tilde h, 1\rangle\ =\ 1.
    \]
Moreover, it holds that
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ \rho^{-n}M^n \ =\ h\tilde h^{\transpose},
\]
where $h\tilde h^{\transpose}$ is the rank-one matrix with entries $(h_x\tilde h_y)_{x,y\in E}$.
\end{thm}

The multitype Galton--Watson process is said to be respectively \emph{subcritical, critical} or \emph{supercritical} if the principal eigenvalue satisfies $\rho < 1$, $\rho=1$ and $\rho>1$.
Moreover, the process defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive_mart}
W_n\ \coloneqq\ \varrho^{-n} \langle Z_n, h\rangle
\end{equation}
is a nonnegative martingale for the filtration $\mathcal{F}_n=\sigma(Z_p,\ p\leq n)$.

\medskip

The continuous time version of the multitype Galton--Watson process introduced in Definition~\ref{def:GW} is called a multitype continuous time branching process. Its dynamic needs an additional parameter $q:E\to \R_+$, such that for every $x\in E$, $q_x$ denotes the branching rate of particles with type $x$. After a branching event, the branching particle with type $x$ is replaced, like in the multitype Galton--Watson process, by a vector of typed offsprings distributed according to $\mathcal{L}_x$. The concept of mean matrix is replaced by the rate matrix, with coordinates
\[
R_{xy}\ \coloneqq\ q_x\big(\E[L_{xy}]- e_x\big).
\]
Denoting the process by $(Z_t)_{t\leq 0}$ and starting from the initial condition $Z_0\in\N^E$,  the mean behavior of the system at time $t$ is given by the nonnegative matrix
\[
\E[Z_t]\ =\ Z_0\exp(t M),
\]
and the Perron--Frobenius applies to the matrix $M=\exp(R)$. In continuous time, the additive martingale is given by $(W_t)_{t \geq 0}$ such that for all $t\geq 0$,
\[
W_t\ \coloneqq\ \e^{-\rho t} \langle Z_t, h\rangle,
\]
where $\rho \in [-1, +\infty )$ is the principal eigenvalue of the matrix $R$.

\paragraph{Limit theorems forward in time.}
   
Limit theorems for multitype Galton--Watson and multitype continuous-time branching processes rely strongly on the Perron--Frobenius theorem and the triplet of principal eigenvalue and eigenvectors provided by the limiting mean dynamics. 
For structured branching processes with a more general set of types, 
the existence of results extending the Perron--Frobenius theorem are also a useful ingredient to deriving the limit dynamics to more general types: Krein--Rutman, \cite{vere-jones_ergodic_1967} for infinite nonnegative matrices
continuous-time structured branching processes, 


As for Cannings models, the finiteness of the variance of the offspring distribution or its power-law divergence has a strong impact on the limiting behaviour. For example, if the process is critical and the variance of the offspring distribution is finite, the Haldane formula gives that the probability of survival of the process is inversely proportional to time, and that on the set of nonextinction, the Yaglom entrance law theorem (see~\cite{Yaglom}) gives that the population grows linearly.

\medskip

When the structured Galton--Watson processes is supercritical, the large population behavior of the process is described precisely by the celebrated Kesten--Stigum theorem~\cite{kesten_limit_1966}.


In monotype Galton--Watson processes, the Kesten--Stigum theorem aims at finding conditions for the non-degeneracy of the a.s.\ limit $W$ of the so-called additive martingale of the process introduced in~\eqref{eq:additive_mart}. There results also relate the non-denegeneracy of $W$ to the extinction event of the process, that is
\[
\mathrm{Ext}\ \coloneqq\ \{\exists n \geq 0,\ Z_n \ =\ 0\}.
\]
In the (finite) multitype setting, the theorem provides in 
addition some asymptotics for type-distribution, \textit{i.e.} the behavior of $\varrho^{-n}Z(n)$).

More precisely, the following theorem is well-known from the seminal work of~\cite{kesten_limit_1966}.
\begin{thm}[Kesten--Stigum theorem]
Let $(Z_n)_{n}$ be multitype branching processes with a finite set of types. If the mean matrix is irreducible and aperiodic, then
\begin{equation*}
   \mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{x,y\in E}L_{xy}\log_+\big(L_{xy}\big)\bigg]<\infty \ \Longleftrightarrow\  \begin{cases}
    \mathbb{E}[W]\ =\ \langle Z_0, h\rangle, \\ \mathbb{P}(W>0)\ >\ 0, \\ \{W=0\}\ =\ \mathrm{Ext},\  \text{up to a null-measure set},
   \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
and the three items on the right-hand side are equivalent.

Moreover, they showed that the normalized distribution of the types in the population satisfies almost-surely that
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Z_n}{\lVert Z_n\rVert}\ =\ W\tilde{h}.
\]
\end{thm}
A short and elegant proof of this result was established in~\citet*{kurtz_conceptual_1997}, building on the spinal change of measure developed by the same authors for single type branching processes (see~\citet*{lyons_pemantle_peres_1995}).

In Chapter~\ref{chp2_ks_countable}, we extend these results to Galton--Watson processes on a countable set of types.
\paragraph{Genealogies of multitype Galton--Watson processes and universal limits.}


The scaling limits of genealogies of Galton--Watson processes are well understood. For a critical single-type Galton--Watson tree with finite offspring variance, conditioned on survival or on large size, the rescaled tree converges to the Brownian continuum random tree. This process was introduced by Aldous~\cite{aldous_continuum_1991, aldous_continuum_1993} and is the universal scaling limit of Galton--Watson processes with finite variance. In the branching litterature, the convergence to the CRT is usually proved through the convergence of the contour or height process to a normalized Brownian excursion \cite{aldous_continuum_1991, le_gall_brownian_1991}. 

This result extends to trees with a type structure. Indeed, for critical irreducible Galton--Watson trees with finitely many types and finite offspring variance, the rescaled tree converges again to the Brownian continuum random tree, up to a constant factor (see \citet{miermont_invariance_2008}). Moreover, \citet{miermont_invariance_2008} proved that the types observed along the tree equilibrate and decouple from the genealogy, being asymptotically distributed according to the stationary distribution of the mean offspring matrix (that is, $\tilde h$ in our notations).

When conditioning branching Markov population model to survive up to a large time $T$ and then sample $k$ individuals uniformly among those alive at that same time, the resulting genealogy is naturally an ultrametric space. For the ultrametric genealogy of the population alive at a fixed (large) generation, a different description is available. Conditional on survival to generation $N$, the genealogy of the extant population, with time rescaled by $N$, converges to the Brownian coalescent point process (CPP). The Brownian CPP is an ultrametric space built from a Poisson point process with intensity $dt\otimes x^{-2}dx$ (see the construction by \citet{popovic_asymptotic_2004}). 
For the critical multitype Galton--Watson process, \citet*{popovic_coalescent_2014} proved that, conditionally on survival, the genealogy of individuals sampled uniformly at a large fixed time converges to the Brownian CPP. 
The CPP is also the genealogy induced by the Feller diffusion, which is forward-in-time scaling limit of the multitype Galton--Watson process. \citet*{7_authors_paper} proved that it can be described as a Kingman coalescent run on a random time change.

She studied a branching Brownian motion with a space-dependent branching rate, negative drift and absorption at the origin, in which the drift is tuned so that the number of particles stays roughly constant. This model serves as a toy model for front expansion dynamics.

Moment methods give a roadmap to the genealogies of multitype branching processes. 
The principle of the methodology is the same as for real-valued random variables: convergence in distribution of a sequence of random variables follows from the convergence of its moments, provided the limit is determined by them. 

For genealogies, the role of the $k$-th moment is played by evaluating a bounded continuous functional of genealogies and the types over all $k$-tuples of individuals alive at sampling time. Convergence of the moments amounts to convergence in the Gromov-weak topology, under an additional moment growth condition (see \citet*[Theorem~4]{Foutel_Schertzer}).
From there, the so-called \emph{many-to-few} formula allows to express the  the $k$-moment (which is a sum over the whole population) in terms of a single tree with $k$ leaves and a biased offspring distribution called the $k$-spine. Thus, the question of the convergence of the genealogies reduces to convergence of the $k$ underlying spines.

The contour function and height function approach of Aldous~\cite{aldous_continuum_1991} and Le Gall~\cite{le_gall_brownian_1991} instead encodes the whole tree as an excursion and proves that this excursion converges. It works well when the reproduction distribution does not depend on types, since in this setting, the encoding is a tractable process (a random walk). However, it breaks down when there is a dependency on the type structure, which makes it inconvenient when studying biology-inspired structured populations. 

In the multitype setting no systematic approach is available \cite{popovic_coalescent_2014}, because the encoding process is no longer Markov or tractable. The moment method does not need such an encoding: it only requires the spinal decomposition, so types of a general (Polish) space enter only through the Markov chain along the spines, and it is well suited to near-critical regimes and to models with strong dependence between types and reproduction, such as the branching process with recombination \cite{Foutel_Schertzer}.




The basic tool is the many-to-few formula of \cite{Harris_Roberts}: particles move in a general type space as Markov processes and branch at a type-dependent rate, and a $k$-spine construction places $k$ marks on the ancestral particle. At each branching event every mark independently and uniformly chooses one offspring to follow, so the integer $k$ is progressively fragmented among the progeny. Under the spine measure, a particle carrying $j$ marks reproduces according to the $j$-th size-biased offspring law, while unmarked particles are unchanged. This expresses $k$-fold sums over particles at time $t$ as an expectation over $k$ dependent spines. In this construction the spine tree depends only on the moments of the offspring law, and in many scaling regimes the bias degenerates in the limit (for instance, the first split of the spine tree stays of order $1$, whereas the most recent common ancestor of the population lives at a time of order $N$).
\cite{Foutel_Schertzer} build the $k$-spine tree differently. Its shape is a coalescent point process (CPP) with i.i.d. branch-time distribution $\nu$ chosen a priori, types evolve along its branches as independent copies of the Doob $h$-transformed Markov chain, and the law of a sample of $k$ individuals from the same generation is recovered through an explicit bias factor. Since $\nu$ is free, it can be tuned as an ansatz for the limiting genealogy so that the bias converges to a non-degenerate limit. Combined with a method of moments in the (marked) Gromov-weak topology, this shows that, after rescaling, the genealogy of the extant population converges to the Brownian CPP, an ultrametric space built from i.i.d. coalescence heights rather than from an explicit tree. They apply it to a branching approximation of the Wright--Fisher model with recombination, where types are subintervals of $(0,R)$, and obtain the Brownian CPP after a logarithmic rescaling of time. The method was extended in \cite{boenkost2022genealogy} to near-critical branching processes in varying environment, where similar spinal and moment arguments give convergence, in the Gromov--Hausdorff--Prohorov topology, to a time-changed Brownian CPP.
When the sample is not pinned to a single generation, for instance when birth times are recorded together with genealogical distances, the ultrametric structure is lost and the natural limit is the continuum random tree (CRT), the universal scaling limit of critical Galton--Watson trees with finite variance. A moment approach to this case was developed in \cite{foutelrodier2024momentapproachconvergencespatial}, where the population is encoded as a bi-metric measure space carrying the tree distance and a birth-time mark, with convergence in a marked extension of the Gromov-weak topology adapted to this non-ultrametric setting.





More generally, when the forward dynamics converges to a continuous-state branching process, one expects the sampled genealogy to converge to the corresponding CPP.
The model of~\cite{julie_tourniaire_branching_2024} falls into this class.
Its forward limit is a CSBP: an $\alpha$-stable one with $\alpha\in(1,2)$ in the semi-pushed regime, on a timescale $N^{\alpha-1}$, and a Feller diffusion in the fully pushed regime. The genealogy of this particle model was proven to converge, in the fully-pushed regime, to the Brownian CPP by~\cite{schertzer2023spectral}, and in the semi-pushed regime to the genealogy of an $\alpha$-stable CSBP (stable CPP) by~\cite{foutel-rodier_convergence_2024}. Both articles rely on a moment-based methodology to derive convergence of the genealogies.
